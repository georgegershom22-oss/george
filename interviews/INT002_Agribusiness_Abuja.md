# Interview Transcript - INT002
**Participant**: Female, 38, Agribusiness Sector, Abuja
**Company**: GreenFields Agro (5-15 employees, 20-50M NGN annual revenue)
**Interview Date**: March 18, 2024
**Duration**: 65 minutes

## Innovation Journey Narrative

**Q: Tell me about your business and your background.**

A: I'm the managing director of GreenFields Agro. We're a small-scale agricultural enterprise that started in 2012. We focus on vegetable farming, particularly tomatoes, peppers, and leafy greens. I have a Master's degree in Agricultural Economics, but I learned most of what I know about farming from my grandmother, who was a traditional farmer.

We started with just 2 hectares of land, and now we have 15 hectares across three locations in the FCT. We employ about 12 people, mostly local farmers and some university graduates who are interested in modern agriculture.

**Q: Can you describe a specific innovation you adopted that significantly changed your business?**

A: The biggest innovation for us was adopting precision agriculture technologies in 2020. This included soil sensors, automated irrigation systems, and mobile apps for farm management. Before this, we were farming the traditional way - relying on experience, weather patterns, and manual labor.

The decision came after we lost almost 40% of our tomato crop in 2019 due to irregular watering and pest infestation. We were using traditional irrigation methods, and it was very labor-intensive and inefficient. I realized we needed to modernize if we wanted to compete and grow.

**Q: What were the main challenges you faced during this adoption?**

A: The financial challenge was enormous. The initial investment was about 3.5 million NGN, which was almost our entire annual profit at that time. We had to take a loan from the Bank of Agriculture, but the interest rate was 15%, which was quite high.

The technical challenge was even bigger. None of my staff knew how to use these technologies. I had to learn everything myself first, then train my team. The learning curve was steep, and there were many frustrating moments when the systems didn't work as expected.

Another major challenge was the unreliable power supply. The automated irrigation system requires constant electricity, but we have power outages almost daily. We had to invest in solar panels and backup generators, which added another 2 million NGN to our costs.

**Q: How did you overcome these challenges?**

A: For the financial challenge, I approached several agricultural development programs. We got some support from the FADAMA project, which covered about 30% of our initial investment. I also partnered with a local agricultural technology company that provided the equipment on a lease-to-own basis.

For the technical challenges, I enrolled in online courses and attended workshops organized by the Federal Ministry of Agriculture. I also hired a young agricultural engineer on a part-time basis to help with the technical aspects.

The power issue was solved by investing in solar power. It was expensive initially, but it has paid off in the long run. We now have reliable power for our irrigation systems, and we even sell excess power to neighboring farms.

**Q: What impact did this innovation have on your business performance?**

A: The results were remarkable. Our crop yield increased by about 60% in the first year. We went from producing 8 tons of tomatoes per hectare to 13 tons per hectare. The quality also improved significantly - we could now produce premium-grade vegetables that commanded higher prices in the market.

Our water usage decreased by about 40% because the automated irrigation system was much more efficient than manual watering. This was particularly important because water is scarce in our area.

Labor costs also reduced. We went from needing 8 people for irrigation to just 2 people to monitor the automated systems. This allowed us to redirect our labor to other important activities like harvesting and packaging.

**Q: How did this affect your relationships with customers and suppliers?**

A: Customer relationships improved dramatically. We could now guarantee consistent quality and supply, which was very important for our buyers. We started supplying to some high-end restaurants and supermarkets in Abuja that we couldn't supply before because we couldn't guarantee consistent quality.

We also started using mobile apps to communicate with our customers. They could place orders directly through the app, and we could provide real-time updates on crop status and delivery schedules.

With suppliers, we had to find new suppliers for the technology components, but we also maintained relationships with our traditional suppliers for things like seeds and fertilizers. The technology actually helped us negotiate better prices with our suppliers because we could provide them with detailed data about our needs.

**Q: What role did government policies play in your innovation adoption?**

A: Government policies were both helpful and frustrating. On the positive side, the government's emphasis on agricultural modernization created awareness and some support programs. We benefited from the FADAMA project and some tax incentives for agricultural technology investments.

However, the bureaucracy was overwhelming. Getting approvals and accessing support programs took months, and the requirements were often unrealistic for small-scale farmers like us. For example, they required detailed business plans and financial projections that were difficult for us to prepare.

The government's import policies also affected us. Most of the precision agriculture equipment had to be imported, and the import duties were very high. This made the technology much more expensive than it should have been.

**Q: What were the biggest barriers you faced?**

A: The biggest barrier was access to finance. Banks are very reluctant to lend to agricultural businesses, especially small-scale ones. The few that do lend charge very high interest rates and require collateral that most farmers don't have.

Another major barrier was the lack of technical support. When our equipment broke down, we had to wait weeks for technicians to come from Lagos or even abroad. This downtime was very costly for us.

The infrastructure challenges were also significant. Poor roads made it difficult to transport our produce, and unreliable internet connectivity made it difficult to use the mobile apps effectively.

**Q: What advice would you give to other agricultural SMEs considering similar innovations?**

A: First, start small and learn gradually. Don't try to adopt all technologies at once. We started with just the irrigation system and gradually added other components.

Second, invest in training and capacity building. Technology is only useful if your team knows how to use it effectively.

Third, build relationships with other farmers and agricultural organizations. We learned a lot from other farmers who had already adopted similar technologies.

Fourth, be prepared for setbacks and challenges. Innovation adoption in agriculture is particularly challenging because you're dealing with biological systems that don't always behave predictably.

**Q: How has this innovation affected your long-term business strategy?**

A: This innovation has completely changed our business strategy. We're no longer just a traditional farm; we're now positioning ourselves as a technology-enabled agricultural enterprise. We're planning to expand into agricultural consulting and technology services for other farmers.

We're also exploring opportunities in agri-tech, such as developing mobile apps for small-scale farmers and providing precision agriculture services. This could be a significant new revenue stream for us.

The innovation has also made us more attractive to investors and development partners. We're now in discussions with some international organizations about potential partnerships and funding opportunities.

**Q: What are your biggest concerns about the future?**

A: My biggest concern is the sustainability of these technologies. They require constant maintenance and updates, which can be expensive. I'm also concerned about the rapid pace of technological change - will the technologies we've invested in become obsolete quickly?

Another concern is the increasing competition. As more farmers adopt these technologies, the competitive advantage might diminish. We need to keep innovating to stay ahead.

**Q: Is there anything else you'd like to add?**

A: Innovation adoption in agriculture is not just about technology; it's about changing mindsets and practices. Many traditional farmers are resistant to change, and it takes time and effort to convince them of the benefits.

I think the government needs to do more to support small-scale farmers in adopting modern technologies. The current support is insufficient and often inaccessible to the farmers who need it most.

Also, I believe there's a need for more local content in agricultural technology. Most of the technologies we use are imported, which makes them expensive and difficult to maintain. We need to develop more local solutions that are appropriate for our specific conditions and challenges.

## Interview Conclusion
This interview revealed the unique challenges and opportunities in agricultural innovation adoption, highlighting the importance of government support, access to finance, and the need for appropriate technology solutions for the Nigerian context.
