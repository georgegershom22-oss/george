FOCUS GROUP DISCUSSION TRANSCRIPT
===================================
FGD ID: FGD002
Sector: Agribusiness
Date: October 13, 2024
Location: Ibadan, Nigeria
Duration: 85 minutes
Participants: 7 SME Owners/Managers

PARTICIPANTS:
- P1: Alhaji Musa (Rice processing, 40 employees)
- P2: Mrs. Blessing (Poultry farm, 25 employees)
- P3: Chief Okonkwo (Palm oil processing, 35 employees)
- P4: Hajiya Fatima (Groundnut processing, 20 employees)
- P5: Mr. Timothy (Cassava processing, 30 employees)
- P6: Ms. Rose (Vegetable farming, 15 employees)
- P7: Pastor John (Fish farming, 18 employees)

[START OF TRANSCRIPT]

MODERATOR: Good morning, everyone. Thank you for joining us to discuss innovation adoption in agribusiness. Let's start with the main challenges you face when trying to modernize your operations.

P1 (Alhaji Musa): The biggest challenge is finance. Banks don't understand agriculture. They want collateral worth 200% of the loan. My rice farm is worth millions, but they want buildings in Lagos.

P2 (Mrs. Blessing): Exactly! And when they do give loans, it's 18-25% interest. How can I innovate when loan repayment takes all my profit?

P5 (Timothy): But even when you have money, getting the right technology is wahala. I ordered cassava processing equipment from China. Six months to arrive, then we discovered it's not suitable for our cassava variety.

P3 (Chief Okonkwo): That's why I prefer local fabrication. Yes, it's not as sophisticated, but at least the fabricator understands our conditions and can maintain it.

MODERATOR: Interesting point about local versus foreign technology. Can you elaborate?

P6 (Rose): Foreign technology assumes stable power, good roads, skilled operators. We don't have these luxuries. I bought a greenhouse from Israel - beautiful technology. But who maintains it when it breaks?

P7 (Pastor John): I had similar experience with automated fish feeders from Denmark. When it broke, the technician they sent charged $2,000 just to come look at it. Not even to fix it!

P4 (Hajiya Fatima): This is why we need technology transfer, not just importation. Teach us to build and maintain these things ourselves.

P1 (Alhaji Musa): But our people resist learning. I sent five workers for training on modern rice milling. Only two took it seriously. Others said, "We've been doing this for 20 years, what can machine teach us?"

MODERATOR: How do you handle resistance to change from workers and even fellow farmers?

P2 (Mrs. Blessing): Education by demonstration. When I introduced automated egg collection, workers feared job loss. I showed them they'd move from collecting eggs to managing data, earning more. Resistance reduced.

P5 (Timothy): Money talks. When neighboring farmers saw my income triple after mechanization, they started asking questions. Success is the best evangelist.

P3 (Chief Okonkwo): But we must be sensitive. These are generational practices. My father processed palm oil manually. When I introduced machines, I still kept one traditional line. Respect for heritage matters.

P7 (Pastor John): Young people are more open. I deliberately hire young graduates. They embrace technology and influence older workers.

MODERATOR: What about market-related challenges to innovation?

P4 (Hajiya Fatima): Nigerians are price-sensitive. I invested in modern packaging for my groundnut oil. Beautiful bottles, proper labeling. Customers still prefer to buy in reused bottles because it's cheaper.

P6 (Rose): Export markets appreciate innovation more. My hydroponically grown vegetables are exported to UK. Locally, people say "it's not natural" and won't pay premium.

P1 (Alhaji Musa): The middlemen are another problem. They prefer status quo because innovation threatens their control. When I started selling directly to retailers using digital platforms, they organized boycotts.

P3 (Chief Okonkwo): Off-takers make promises they don't keep. A company promised to buy all my palm oil if I upgraded my processing. I took loans, upgraded, then they disappeared.

MODERATOR: How do infrastructure challenges specifically affect agricultural innovation?

P2 (Mrs. Blessing): Cold chain is non-existent. I can produce 10,000 eggs daily, but without refrigerated transport, I'm limited to local markets. Innovation in production is useless without storage and transport infrastructure.

P5 (Timothy): Roads! How do you move modern equipment to farms when roads are death traps? I spent 2 million Naira repairing the access road to my farm just to bring in new machines.

P7 (Pastor John): Water supply is critical for aquaculture innovation. Government promises irrigation, delivers nothing. We dig boreholes, but water table is dropping yearly.

P6 (Rose): Don't forget electricity. My greenhouse needs consistent power for climate control. Generator costs make my vegetables more expensive than imports. How is that sustainable?

MODERATOR: What role has government support played in your innovation efforts?

P1 (Alhaji Musa): Government talks plenty but does little. Anchor Borrowers Programme looked good on paper. In practice, the money came late, inputs were substandard, and repayment terms changed mid-cycle.

P4 (Hajiya Fatima): Some interventions help. NIRSAL insurance gave me confidence to expand. When flood destroyed my crop, insurance covered 70% of losses.

P3 (Chief Okonkwo): The problem is politics. Every new government cancels previous programs. No continuity. How do you plan innovation with such uncertainty?

P2 (Mrs. Blessing): Extension services are dead. When last did you see an extension officer? We're on our own, learning from YouTube and making expensive mistakes.

P5 (Timothy): But some states are trying. Ogun State's agricultural program is impressive. They provide land, initial infrastructure, and technical support. Other states should copy.

MODERATOR: What successful innovation strategies have worked for you?

P7 (Pastor John): Cooperative approach works. Five of us fish farmers pooled resources to buy expensive equipment we share. Reduces individual cost and risk.

P6 (Rose): Starting small and scaling gradually. I started with one greenhouse, perfected the system, then expanded. Many fail because they go too big too fast.

P1 (Alhaji Musa): Value addition is key. Instead of selling raw rice, I now package different grades. Revenue increased 60% with same production volume.

P2 (Mrs. Blessing): Integration helps. I now produce my own feed instead of buying. Controlling the value chain improves margins and quality.

P3 (Chief Okonkwo): Digital platforms for market linkage. I found buyers in Lagos and Abuja through online agricultural marketplaces. Better prices, consistent demand.

P4 (Hajiya Fatima): Contract farming with guaranteed buyers. Removes market uncertainty and provides capital for innovation.

MODERATOR: How can agribusiness SMEs better collaborate for innovation?

P5 (Timothy): Knowledge sharing is crucial. When I discover a disease-resistant cassava variety, I should share with others. We're not really competing; the market is huge.

P7 (Pastor John): Joint procurement of inputs and equipment. Bulk buying reduces costs significantly.

P1 (Alhaji Musa): Farmer associations need strengthening. Together, we have bargaining power with government and buyers.

P6 (Rose): Shared processing facilities make sense. Not everyone needs their own factory. Centralized facilities with modern equipment benefit everyone.

P2 (Mrs. Blessing): Peer learning networks. Visiting each other's farms, sharing experiences. Better than waiting for government extension services.

MODERATOR: What specific support would accelerate innovation adoption?

P3 (Chief Okonkwo): Long-term, low-interest loans. Agriculture has long gestation periods. We need patient capital at single-digit rates.

P4 (Hajiya Fatima): Practical training centers. Not theoretical agriculture, but hands-on experience with modern equipment and techniques.

P1 (Alhaji Musa): Market guarantees. If government guarantees to buy certain quantities, we can invest in innovation confidently.

P7 (Pastor John): Research institutions should work with farmers. They develop innovations in labs that don't work on farms. Collaborate with us from the start.

P6 (Rose): Infrastructure! Rural roads, electricity, irrigation. Without these, other innovations are cosmetic.

P2 (Mrs. Blessing): Subsidized technology for genuine farmers. Not political subsidies that go to non-farmers, but targeted support for serious agribusiness.

P5 (Timothy): Policy consistency. Stop changing agricultural policies with every minister. Give us predictability.

MODERATOR: Looking forward, what innovations excite you most?

P1 (Alhaji Musa): Blockchain for traceability. Imagine customers scanning QR codes to see exactly where their rice was grown, when it was harvested. Premium pricing for transparency.

P6 (Rose): Climate-smart agriculture. Drought-resistant varieties, precision irrigation. Climate change is real; we must adapt.

P7 (Pastor John): Aquaponics - combining fish farming with vegetable production. Efficient use of resources, higher yields.

P2 (Mrs. Blessing): Artificial intelligence for disease prediction. Imagine knowing a poultry disease outbreak is likely and preventing it.

P3 (Chief Okonkwo): Renewable energy for processing. Solar-powered mills would transform our cost structure.

P4 (Hajiya Fatima): Mobile apps connecting farmers directly to consumers. Cut out middlemen, increase farmer income.

P5 (Timothy): Biotechnology for improved varieties. Cassava that's naturally fortified with vitamins. Address malnutrition while farming.

MODERATOR: Final thoughts on accelerating innovation in Nigerian agribusiness?

P1 (Alhaji Musa): Change the narrative. Agriculture isn't for failures. It's sophisticated business requiring innovation. Attract young, educated people.

P2 (Mrs. Blessing): Support women farmers specifically. We face additional barriers but often achieve better results when given opportunity.

P3 (Chief Okonkwo): Learn from other African countries. Kenya's agricultural innovation is impressive. What are they doing right?

P7 (Pastor John): Make agriculture profitable. When farmers make money, innovation follows naturally.

P6 (Rose): Environmental sustainability must be part of innovation. We're destroying our soil and water. Technology should help us farm smarter, not just bigger.

P4 (Hajiya Fatima): Include smallholder farmers. Innovation shouldn't be only for big agribusiness. The small farmers feed Nigeria.

P5 (Timothy): Political will is essential. Leaders must genuinely prioritize agricultural transformation, not just campaign promises.

MODERATOR: Thank you all for your valuable insights and experiences.

[END OF TRANSCRIPT]

Key Themes Identified:
1. Access to affordable, patient capital
2. Technology adaptation to local conditions
3. Infrastructure deficits (roads, power, cold chain)
4. Market access and price sensitivity
5. Resistance to change and generational practices
6. Weak extension services and knowledge transfer
7. Policy inconsistency and political interference
8. Value addition and integration strategies
9. Cooperative approaches and resource sharing
10. Climate change adaptation needs
