SEMI-STRUCTURED INTERVIEW TRANSCRIPT
Participant: P002 - Fatima Hassan
Business: GreenHarvest Agro (Organic Farming & Processing)
Date: March 18, 2024
Duration: 58 minutes
Location: Kano, Nigeria
Interviewer: Dr. Sarah Okeke

[INNOVATION JOURNEY]

Interviewer: Tell me about your decision to adopt precision agriculture technologies in your farming operations.

Fatima: Subhanallah, it's been quite a journey. I started GreenHarvest in 2019 with just 5 hectares of land, growing organic vegetables. Traditional farming methods, you know - relying on experience, weather patterns, and Allah's blessings.

But by 2022, I was struggling. Climate change made weather unpredictable. Sometimes too much rain, sometimes drought. I was losing crops and money. My yields were inconsistent - one season I'd harvest 3 tons of tomatoes per hectare, next season maybe 1.5 tons from the same land.

The turning point came when I attended an agricultural fair in Abuja. There was this young man demonstrating soil sensors and drone technology for farming. At first, I thought, "This is too advanced for a small farmer like me." But when he showed me how a farmer in Kaduna increased his maize yield by 35% using these tools, I became interested.

Interviewer: What specific technologies did you decide to adopt?

Fatima: I started with soil pH and moisture sensors. Simple devices that you put in the ground to tell you exactly what your soil needs. Before this, I was guessing. "Maybe the plants need more water, maybe more fertilizer." Now I have data.

Then I added a weather monitoring station - it gives me precise rainfall, temperature, and humidity data for my specific location. Not the general weather forecast, but what's happening on my farm.

The biggest investment was a small agricultural drone for crop monitoring. It cost me 800,000 naira, but it can survey my entire farm in 30 minutes and identify problem areas before I can see them with my eyes.

Interviewer: What were the biggest hurdles in adopting these technologies?

Fatima: Ya Allah, so many challenges! First was the cost. As a woman in agriculture, getting loans is already difficult. Banks look at you like you don't know what you're doing. When I told them I wanted money for "farming gadgets," they were even more skeptical.

Second was the technical knowledge. I studied Agricultural Science, but this was different. Understanding how to interpret sensor data, how to operate the drone, how to use the mobile apps - it was overwhelming at first.

Third was the infrastructure. The sensors need internet connectivity to send data to my phone. In our rural area, network coverage is poor. Sometimes I have to drive 5 kilometers to get good signal to check my farm data.

But the biggest challenge was social. Other farmers in my community thought I was showing off. "Fatima thinks she's too good for traditional farming," they would say. Some even said it was un-Islamic to rely on machines instead of trusting in Allah's provision.

Interviewer: How did you overcome these obstacles?

Fatima: For funding, I joined a women's cooperative. We pooled resources and applied for a group loan from the Bank of Agriculture. It was easier to get approved as a group.

For the technical skills, I found a young agricultural engineer in Kano who became my consultant. He trained me and my two workers for one week. He also set up a WhatsApp group where farmers using similar technology share experiences and solutions.

For the connectivity issue, I negotiated with MTN to install a booster mast near our farming community. I convinced five other progressive farmers to contribute, and MTN agreed because it would serve multiple customers.

The social challenge required patience and results. When my neighbors saw my tomato yield increase from 2.5 tons to 4.2 tons per hectare in one season, the criticism reduced. Now some of them ask me to help them get the same technology.

Interviewer: How has this innovation affected your business performance?

Fatima: Alhamdulillah, the transformation has been remarkable. My crop yields increased by an average of 40% across all vegetables. But more importantly, the quality improved. My tomatoes are more uniform, my leafy vegetables stay fresh longer.

Revenue-wise, I went from making about 8 million naira annually to 12 million naira. But the real benefit is predictability. Before, farming was gambling - you plant and hope. Now, I can predict with 80% accuracy what my harvest will be.

Cost management improved significantly. I reduced fertilizer usage by 25% because I only apply what the soil sensors indicate is needed. Water usage dropped by 30% because I know exactly when and how much to irrigate.

Customer relationships changed completely. I now supply three major supermarkets in Kano and Abuja because I can guarantee consistent quality and quantity. They know that when Fatima promises 500 kg of organic tomatoes on a specific date, they will get exactly that.

Interviewer: What role did government support play?

Fatima: Limited, but there were some helpful initiatives. The Kano State Agricultural Development Program provided subsidized fertilizers, which helped reduce costs. The Central Bank's Anchor Borrowers Program connected me with off-takers, guaranteeing a market for my produce.

But there's no specific support for agricultural technology adoption. The government talks about modernizing agriculture, but there are no grants or tax incentives for buying precision farming equipment.

What helped indirectly was the improved road network. The federal government rehabilitated the road from our farm to the main market, making it easier to transport produce and reducing post-harvest losses.

Interviewer: Tell me about any unexpected challenges or benefits.

Fatima: Unexpected challenge: Data management. These sensors generate so much information - soil moisture every hour, weather data every 30 minutes. At first, I was overwhelmed. I had to learn how to analyze trends, not just look at individual readings.

Another surprise was maintenance. These devices need regular calibration and cleaning. In our dusty environment, sensors can give wrong readings if not properly maintained.

Unexpected benefit: I became a knowledge hub for other farmers. Agricultural extension officers now bring visitors to my farm to see "modern farming in action." This networking led to new business opportunities - I now sell seedlings and offer consulting services to other farmers.

Also, my children became more interested in agriculture. My 16-year-old son now helps me analyze the data and operate the drone. He's considering studying Agricultural Engineering instead of leaving farming like most young people.

Interviewer: How has this affected your position in the agricultural value chain?

Fatima: I moved from being just a producer to being an integrated agribusiness. I now process some of my vegetables into dried products and organic fertilizer. The precision farming data helps me optimize the entire process.

I also started contract farming - other farmers grow specific crops for me using my technology and methods. I provide the seeds, technology, and knowledge; they provide land and labor. This expanded my effective farm size from 5 hectares to 25 hectares without buying more land.

Interviewer: What advice would you give to other agricultural SMEs?

Fatima: Start with what you can afford. You don't need to buy everything at once. I started with just soil sensors for 50,000 naira. When I saw the benefits, I gradually added more technology.

Join or form cooperatives. Individual farmers can't afford these technologies alone, but groups can. Also, technology companies are more willing to provide training and support to groups.

Don't ignore the social aspect. Take time to explain to your community what you're doing and why. Their support or resistance can make or break your innovation journey.

Most importantly, remember that technology is a tool, not a replacement for good farming practices. You still need to understand your crops, your soil, your climate. Technology just makes you more precise and efficient.

[FUTURE PLANS]

Interviewer: What's next for your innovation journey?

Fatima: I'm exploring blockchain technology for traceability. Consumers, especially in urban areas, want to know where their food comes from. If I can provide a digital record of how each tomato was grown - what fertilizer was used, when it was harvested - I can charge premium prices.

I'm also looking at solar-powered irrigation systems. With climate change, rainfall is becoming more unpredictable. Having reliable irrigation powered by renewable energy would make me completely independent of weather variations.

The goal is to become a model for sustainable, technology-driven agriculture in Northern Nigeria. If a woman farmer in Kano can do it, others can too.
