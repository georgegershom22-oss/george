INTERVIEW TRANSCRIPT
=====================
Participant ID: INT001
Name: Adebayo Ogundimu
Company: TechHub Solutions Ltd
Date: September 15, 2024
Duration: 52 minutes
Location: Lagos, Nigeria

[START OF TRANSCRIPT]

INTERVIEWER: Good morning, Mr. Ogundimu. Thank you for taking the time to speak with me today about innovation in your business. Before we begin, I want to assure you that everything we discuss will remain confidential, and your responses will only be used for research purposes. May I have your permission to record this conversation?

ADEBAYO: Good morning. Yes, you have my permission to record. I'm happy to share our experience.

INTERVIEWER: Excellent. Let's start with some background. Could you tell me about your business - what do you do and how long have you been operating?

ADEBAYO: TechHub Solutions has been operational for 8 years now. We started in 2016 as a small IT consulting firm with just myself and two other partners. Today, we provide comprehensive IT solutions - from software development to system integration, cybersecurity, and now cloud services. We primarily serve medium to large enterprises across West Africa, though 70% of our clients are here in Nigeria.

INTERVIEWER: That's impressive growth. What's your role in the company?

ADEBAYO: I'm the CEO and co-founder. I oversee strategic direction, but I'm still very hands-on with major client relationships and innovation initiatives. In a business our size, you can't afford to be too removed from operations.

INTERVIEWER: How many employees do you currently have?

ADEBAYO: We have 35 full-time employees now, plus about 10-15 contractors depending on project needs. It's been quite a journey from our initial team of 3.

INTERVIEWER: What would you say is your main market - local, national, or international?

ADEBAYO: I'd say 60% national, 30% regional West Africa, and 10% international. We've been pushing to expand regionally, especially into Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire. The international component is mostly diaspora-owned businesses wanting to invest back home.

INTERVIEWER: Now, let's talk about your innovation journey. Can you tell me the story of when you decided to adopt the cloud-based ERP system?

ADEBAYO: Ah, that's an interesting story. It was about two years ago, early 2022. We were growing rapidly - we'd just landed three major contracts, and our old systems were creaking. We were using a combination of spreadsheets, a basic accounting software, and lots of manual processes. I remember one Friday evening, my operations manager came to me almost in tears. She said, "Oga, we can't continue like this. We're losing track of projects, invoicing is delayed, and we can't even properly track our inventory of equipment."

That weekend, I couldn't sleep. I realized we were at a crossroads - either we modernize or we start losing clients due to inefficiency. The trigger was really when we almost missed a major deliverable for First Bank because of poor internal communication and project tracking. That was our wake-up call.

INTERVIEWER: Who was involved in the decision-making process?

ADEBAYO: I pulled together what I called our "Innovation Committee" - myself, the operations manager, our lead developer, the finance manager, and surprisingly, two of our junior staff who were very tech-savvy. I wanted diverse perspectives. We spent three months evaluating options, talking to vendors, and most importantly, talking to other SMEs who had gone through similar transitions.

INTERVIEWER: Walk me through the implementation process. What were the phases?

ADEBAYO: [Laughs] It was a rollercoaster! Phase one was selection and customization - that took about 2 months. We went with Microsoft Dynamics 365 because of its flexibility and cloud nature. Phase two was data migration and system setup - another 2 months. This was painful because our data was scattered everywhere. Phase three was training and parallel running - we ran both old and new systems for 3 months. Phase four was full deployment and optimization, which honestly, we're still in.

The critical moment came in month 4 when we had to make a decision - our accounting team was struggling and wanted to revert to the old system. I had to make a tough call to push forward. I personally spent weekends training with them, showing them the long-term benefits.

INTERVIEWER: What were the biggest hurdles you faced, and how did you navigate them?

ADEBAYO: Eh, where do I start? First was cost - the initial investment was about 15 million Naira, which was huge for us. We had to take a bank loan and use some of our reserves. I negotiated a payment plan with the vendor and convinced our board that this was existential for us.

Second was resistance to change. Some of our senior staff who had been with us for years felt threatened. They were comfortable with the old ways. I had to do a lot of one-on-one conversations, assuring them that no one would lose their jobs, that this would actually make their work easier. We also instituted a "digital champion" program where those who adapted quickly would mentor others and get bonuses.

Third was technical challenges. Our internet infrastructure wasn't reliable enough initially. We had to invest in backup internet connections, upgrade our office WiFi, and even provide internet allowances for remote workers. The power situation in Lagos didn't help - we upgraded our generator and inverter systems.

Fourth was the learning curve. The system was sophisticated, and most of our staff weren't prepared for it. We underestimated training needs. We ended up hiring an external consultant for three months to provide intensive training.

INTERVIEWER: How has this innovation specifically affected your sales and revenue?

ADEBAYO: The impact has been remarkable. Our revenue has grown by 40% in the past year alone. But it's not just growth - it's the quality of growth. We can now handle larger, more complex projects. Our proposal turnaround time dropped from 2 weeks to 3 days. Our invoicing is now automated, so cash flow improved by 30%. We used to have payment delays because invoices would get lost or forgotten. Now everything is tracked.

The CRM component has been a game-changer for sales. We can track every client interaction, predict client needs, and our sales team is 50% more productive. We won two major contracts specifically because we could demonstrate our operational sophistication during the bidding process.

INTERVIEWER: What about operational costs?

ADEBAYO: Initially, costs went up by about 20% due to subscriptions, training, and infrastructure. But after 8 months, we started seeing savings. We reduced operational costs by 25% within the first year. How? Less paperwork, fewer errors, faster project completion, better resource allocation. We also eliminated two expensive software subscriptions because the ERP integrated those functions.

Most importantly, we avoided the cost of inefficiency. Before, we'd sometimes have three people working on the same task without knowing it. Or equipment would be purchased multiple times because we didn't have proper inventory tracking. Those hidden costs were killing us.

INTERVIEWER: How about customer relationships?

ADEBAYO: This is where I get excited! Customer satisfaction scores went up by 35%. We now have a client portal where they can track project progress in real-time. No more endless phone calls asking for updates. Our response time to client queries dropped from 24 hours to 2 hours on average.

We've also been able to offer new services. Because we have better data analytics, we can provide clients with insights about their IT usage, predict potential issues, and offer proactive solutions. Three of our biggest clients increased their contracts with us specifically because of these new capabilities.

INTERVIEWER: Were there any unexpected outcomes - positive or negative?

ADEBAYO: Several surprises! Positive: Employee satisfaction actually went up after the initial resistance. Our employee survey showed 70% felt more empowered and professional. We became attractive to top talent - we hired three excellent developers who specifically mentioned our modern systems as a draw.

Another positive surprise was that it forced us to document all our processes. This standardization made us ISO-ready, and we're now pursuing ISO certification, which will open more doors.

Negative: We didn't anticipate the cyber security implications. Being cloud-based made us more vulnerable to attacks. We had one scare with a phishing attempt that could have been catastrophic. We had to invest another 5 million in security measures and training.

Also, we became somewhat dependent on stable internet. During one major network outage in Lagos, we were partially paralyzed for six hours. That taught us to have better contingency plans.

INTERVIEWER: How do you measure the success of this innovation in your business?

ADEBAYO: We track several KPIs: System adoption rate - we're at 95% now. Project completion time - reduced by 35%. Customer satisfaction - up from 7.2 to 8.7 out of 10. Employee productivity - measured by revenue per employee, up 30%. Error rates in invoicing and project delivery - down 80%.

But beyond numbers, I measure success by our capability to take on projects we couldn't before. Last month, we won a 200 million Naira contract that required real-time reporting capabilities. Old us couldn't have even bid for it.

INTERVIEWER: What external support did you receive, if any?

ADEBAYO: The Bank of Industry gave us a loan at 9% interest under their SME digitalization fund - that was crucial. NITDA [National Information Technology Development Agency] provided free consultation on best practices. The Lagos State Employment Trust Fund partially subsidized our staff training costs.

Our industry association, Nigeria Computer Society, connected us with other SMEs who had gone through similar transformations. Those peer learning sessions were invaluable. Microsoft Nigeria also provided discounted licenses for the first year as part of their SME support program.

But I must say, government support could be much better. The bureaucracy to access these funds was almost overwhelming. It took us 4 months to get the BOI loan approved. Many SMEs give up.

INTERVIEWER: What role did your employees play in the adoption process?

ADEBAYO: Critical! Without them, we would have failed. Our younger employees became natural ambassadors. They created WhatsApp groups to share tips, made video tutorials in pidgin English for better understanding, and celebrated small wins.

Our operations manager became the project champion. She would stay late to help struggling colleagues, created cheat sheets, and constantly reminded everyone of the "why" behind the change.

But we also had to let two employees go - they just couldn't adapt and were becoming toxic, constantly complaining and discouraging others. That was tough but necessary.

INTERVIEWER: If you were advising another SME owner about innovation adoption, what would you tell them?

ADEBAYO: First, start with why. Be clear about the problem you're solving. Don't adopt technology for technology's sake. Second, budget twice what you think you need - there are always hidden costs. Third, invest heavily in change management. The technology is the easy part; changing mindsets is hard.

Fourth, get your team involved early. Don't surprise them with changes. Fifth, find a good implementation partner. Don't try to do it alone. Sixth, be patient but persistent. You'll want to quit multiple times. Don't. The payoff is worth it.

Finally, document everything. Your learnings, processes, challenges. You'll need to reference them, and they become valuable institutional knowledge.

INTERVIEWER: What's next for your business in terms of innovation?

ADEBAYO: We're looking at AI integration now. We want to use AI for predictive analytics, automated customer support, and code review. We're also exploring blockchain for secure document management for our financial services clients.

But we're being more strategic now. We have an innovation roadmap for the next 3 years. We've learned that innovation needs to be continuous but measured, not sporadic and reactive.

INTERVIEWER: What changes in the business environment would make innovation easier for SMEs like yours?

ADEBAYO: Where do I begin? First, infrastructure - we need reliable power and internet. It's 2024, and we're still dealing with power cuts. That's unacceptable. Second, access to affordable finance. Interest rates are killing innovation. Many SMEs have great ideas but can't afford the 20-25% banks are charging.

Third, digital literacy programs. The government should massively invest in training programs. Not everyone can afford private training. Fourth, tax incentives for innovation. Why not give tax breaks to companies that invest in digital transformation?

Fifth, reduce bureaucracy. Make it easier to access government support programs. Create one-stop shops for SME innovation support. Sixth, enforce digital payment policies. If government agencies all paid electronically and on time, it would improve cash flow for SMEs and encourage digital adoption.

Finally, create innovation hubs in every state, not just Lagos and Abuja. Provide spaces where SMEs can access technology, training, and mentorship at subsidized rates.

INTERVIEWER: This has been incredibly insightful, Mr. Ogundimu. Any final thoughts?

ADEBAYO: Just that innovation is not optional for Nigerian SMEs anymore. The world is moving fast, and we either adapt or become irrelevant. But it's not easy, especially in our environment. It takes courage, resources, and persistence. Government and large corporations need to support SMEs more. We employ millions and drive the economy, but we're often left to struggle alone.

But I'm optimistic. Nigerian entrepreneurs are resilient and creative. With the right support and mindset, we can compete globally. Our digital transformation has shown me what's possible. If we can do it, others can too.

INTERVIEWER: Thank you so much for your time and candid responses.

ADEBAYO: You're welcome. I hope this helps other SMEs on their innovation journey.
