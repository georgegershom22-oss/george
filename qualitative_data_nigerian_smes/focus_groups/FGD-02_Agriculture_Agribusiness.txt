FOCUS GROUP DISCUSSION TRANSCRIPT
===================================

FGD ID: FGD-02
Sector: Agriculture & Agribusiness SMEs
Location: IITA Conference Center, Ibadan, Oyo State
Date: September 15, 2024
Duration: 128 minutes
Facilitator: Dr. Emeka Nwachukwu
Number of Participants: 8

PARTICIPANTS:
1. Alhaji Musa (M, 56) - Crop Farming, Kano - Basic mechanization
2. Mrs. Chidinma (F, 38) - Agritech, Enugu - IoT sensors
3. Samuel (M, 44) - Poultry, Ogun - Automated systems
4. Fatima (F, 41) - Dairy, Jos - Cold chain
5. Oke (M, 49) - Fish farming, Delta - Water monitoring
6. Bunmi (F, 35) - Vegetable farming, Oyo - Greenhouse tech
7. Yusuf (M, 52) - Rice milling, Kebbi - Processing machinery
8. Chiamaka (F, 33) - Food processing, Anambra - Packaging automation

---

FACILITATOR: Thank you for coming. Agriculture is Nigeria's backbone but often seen as "traditional" sector. Yet all of you are adopting technologies. Let's start: What motivated you to adopt technology in farming or agribusiness?

ALHAJI MUSA: For me, it was survival. I'm 56 years old. I've been farming since I was young boy following my father. For 40 years, we farmed the same way—manual tilling, manual planting, manual harvesting. But land is getting less productive, labor is getting expensive, young people don't want to farm. I had to change or close down.

In 2019, I bought a tractor—first time! My neighbors thought I was wasting money. "Alhaji, you've farmed without tractor for 40 years, why now?" But that tractor allowed me to prepare 20 hectares in one week. Before, same work took 30 laborers two months. The mathematics was clear.

MRS. CHIDINMA: My story is different. I'm younger generation—I studied Agricultural Economics, worked with NGO doing rural development. I saw the poverty, the struggle of smallholder farmers. I thought, "Technology can change this." 

I started AgroFresh with vision of smart farming—sensors, data, mobile apps. My parents thought I was mad. "You studied at university to play with phones on farm?" [laughs] But I believed in it.

SAMUEL: For me, it was disease outbreak. I lost 3,000 chickens in 2020 to Newcastle disease. I couldn't detect it early because I was monitoring manually. I would visit farm twice daily, but diseases spread fast. After that loss—almost ₦4.5 million—I knew I needed better monitoring. I installed automated feeding and health monitoring sensors.

FATIMA: Similar story. Dairy farming, milk spoils very quickly. Before I had cold storage, I was losing maybe 30% of my milk daily. Either it spoiled before reaching market, or I had to sell cheaply to avoid spoilage. Installing cold room and refrigerated transport changed everything.

FACILITATOR: So technology adoption was driven by specific problems—labor, disease, spoilage. Not just following trends?

OKE: [nods strongly] Exactly! Farmers are practical people. We don't adopt technology for fashion. We adopt when we have problem and technology is the solution.

For me, fish farming, the problem was water quality. Fish are very sensitive—oxygen level, pH, temperature, ammonia. Before, I was testing water manually twice daily. But fish don't operate on human schedule! Water quality can crash at 3 AM, by morning all fish are dead. I lost two ponds that way—₦2.8 million loss.

Now I have sensors that monitor 24/7, alert me on phone if anything is wrong. Even at night, I can respond quickly. Haven't lost a pond since installing system.

BUNMI: I want to add something. For some of us, technology is not just about solving problems but about opportunities. I started greenhouse farming because I saw market opportunity—high-value vegetables year-round, not just seasonal. Traditional open-field farming can't do that. Technology opened new market for me.

YUSUF: And competitive pressure. I'm rice miller in Kebbi. Nigerian rice was losing to imported rice—cheaper, better quality, better packaging. If we local millers don't improve technology, we'll die. Government ban on rice importation helped, but we still need to compete on quality. That's why I bought modern milling machines.

CHIAMAKA: Food processing is same. Consumers want hygienic, professionally packaged products. The days of selling food in old containers are gone. If you want to enter supermarkets, you need proper technology—processing, packaging, labeling. It's not optional.

FACILITATOR: So diverse motivations but common thread—technology as necessary for survival or growth. Now, what are the biggest challenges you face in adopting these technologies?

[Long pause, then several speak at once]

ALHAJI MUSA: Money! [others laugh and nod]

FATIMA: Everything is expensive.

SAMUEL: Capital access is the number one barrier.

FACILITATOR: Clearly funding is an issue. Let's explore this. How expensive, and why is it hard to get funding?

MRS. CHIDINMA: Let me give numbers. IoT sensors for 10 hectares farm—₦1.2 million. Mobile app development—₦800,000. Solar panels for power—₦600,000. Installation and training—₦400,000. Total: ₦3 million. For small farmer who makes maybe ₦5-8 million revenue yearly, that's huge investment.

BUNMI: My greenhouse cost ₦6 million for just one hectare. Without bank loan, impossible for most farmers.

YUSUF: My rice mill machinery from India—₦45 million. I tried getting bank loan. They wanted 30% equity contribution upfront—that's ₦13.5 million cash! Also land title as collateral, plus personal guarantor. Very difficult.

ALHAJI MUSA: In the North, many farmers don't even have land titles. Land is communal or inherited. Banks don't accept that as collateral. So even if farmer wants loan, bank says no.

FATIMA: And interest rates! Bank offered me 24% interest rate. Twenty-four percent! How can dairy farming with 15% profit margin afford 24% loan? The mathematics doesn't work.

OKE: Some of us used cooperative societies—thrift savings groups. Interest is lower, maybe 10-12%, but amounts are small. My cooperative could only lend me ₦500,000. I needed ₦3 million.

CHIAMAKA: I used family money—my uncle in diaspora invested. But not everyone has rich uncle abroad. We need better financing options.

FACILITATOR: Are there government loans or grant programs?

[Cynical laughter from group]

SAMUEL: On paper, yes. In practice, very difficult. I applied to BOI agricultural loan. Six months of documentation, back and forth. Eventually rejected because my business is "too small."

BUNMI: NIRSAL [Nigeria Incentive-Based Risk Sharing System for Agricultural Lending] was supposed to help. I applied in 2021. Still waiting for response in 2024! [laughs bitterly]

MRS. CHIDINMA: There are grants from World Bank programs, AfDB, development partners. Very competitive—thousands applying, few selected. I applied four times, got one grant of ₦2 million. Helpful but not enough.

YUSUF: Government programs favor big farmers or political connected people. Small farmers like us don't have access.

ALHAJI MUSA: Let me be honest—some programs, you need to "settle" somebody to access them. Corruption is there. If you don't pay, you don't get.

FACILITATOR: Beyond funding, what other challenges?

FATIMA: Power! Always power. My cold room needs 24-hour electricity. NEPA gives maybe 6-8 hours daily. I run diesel generator rest of the time. Diesel costs are eating my profits.

OKE: Same here. My water sensors, aerators, feeders—all need electricity. Generator cost in 2023 was ₦1.8 million. That's huge!

MRS. CHIDINMA: Solar is option, but upfront cost is high. I installed solar for my farm—₦4.5 million. It's paying back through diesel savings, but I had to take loan for it.

BUNMI: Greenhouse climate control needs constant power. Without power, temperature rises, crops stress, yield drops. Power failure costs me money every time.

FACILITATOR: What about technical challenges—knowledge, skills, maintenance?

SAMUEL: [raises hand enthusiastically] Big challenge! When I bought automated feeding system, it came with English manual—200 pages! Technical English I don't fully understand. The supplier sent technician for 2 days training. Two days! For complex system.

I learned through trial and error. Made many mistakes—overfed chickens, underfed them, system breakdown. Took me 4 months to really master it.

CHIAMAKA: Maintenance is nightmare. These machines break down. Where do you get spare parts in Nigeria? I have packaging machine from China. When it broke, I had to order spare part from China—took 6 weeks to arrive. Meanwhile, machine is idle, I'm losing production.

YUSUF: That's why I prefer locally fabricated equipment when possible. Yes, Chinese machines are more advanced, but when they break, you're stuck. Local fabricator, you call him, he comes same week.

OKE: But local fabricators can't make everything. For sophisticated electronics—sensors, controllers—you have to import. Then you face customs wahala.

MRS. CHIDINMA: Oh, customs! [others groan] My sensors stayed in Lagos port for 2 months. Demurrage fees, "facilitation fees," documentation. By the time I cleared them, I had paid almost same as the equipment cost!

ALHAJI MUSA: And no technical support. Where are the agricultural extension officers? In my father's time, extension officers visited farms regularly, taught farmers new techniques. Now? I haven't seen extension officer in 5 years. We're on our own.

BUNMI: I learn from YouTube! [laughs] Seriously, YouTube University. I watch greenhouse farming videos from Kenya, India, Israel. That's my agricultural extension.

FATIMA: We need training centers for agricultural technology. Not university theoretical courses—practical, hands-on training. How to operate this machine, how to troubleshoot that system.

FACILITATOR: Let's talk about markets. Has technology helped you access better markets or get better prices?

CHIAMAKA: Absolutely! Before I automated packaging, I was selling to local markets—low prices, seasonal demand, many rejections because of poor packaging quality. Now I supply supermarkets—Shoprite, Justrite, Game Stores. They pay 40% higher prices and pay reliably.

MRS. CHIDINMA: Technology opened export opportunities for me. I can track my production data, show buyers our quality control, certify our processes. We now export to Ghana and Cameroon. Without data and traceability, impossible to export.

SAMUEL: My poultry, I can guarantee uniform quality because of controlled environment and automated systems. Hotels and restaurants prefer my chicken because they know quality is consistent. I charge premium prices.

BUNMI: Greenhouse allows me to grow vegetables off-season. When everyone else is out of supply, I'm the only supplier. I can charge 2-3 times normal price. That's where I make profit.

OKE: But market access is still limited for many of us. Middlemen control markets. Even with good quality fish, I still have to sell through middlemen who take big cut. Technology improved my production, but market structure is still exploitative.

YUSUF: For rice, government policy helps. Border closure on rice importation created market for us. But distribution is challenge. Big traders dominate. Small millers like me struggle to reach retailers directly.

ALHAJI MUSA: Transportation is problem. I can produce tomatoes with irrigation, but transporting them from Kano to Lagos, half spoil because of bad roads and long journey. Technology on the farm is one thing, but post-harvest technology—cold trucks, good roads—is lacking.

FATIMA: Exactly! I have best dairy technology, but the road from Jos to Abuja is terrible. Milk shakes and spoils during transport. We need infrastructure beyond the farm.

FACILITATOR: How about environmental or climate issues? Has technology helped you adapt to climate change?

OKE: Very much! Rainfall patterns are changing. My father's generation could predict rain by looking at clouds, by traditional calendar. Now? Rain comes unexpectedly or doesn't come when expected. 

With irrigation and water monitoring technology, I'm less dependent on rain. I can control water supply to my ponds year-round.

MRS. CHIDINMA: Sensors give me data to make better decisions. I can see soil moisture levels, rainfall patterns over time. I can plan planting based on data, not just guesswork.

BUNMI: Greenhouse protects my crops from extreme weather—too much rain, too much sun, hailstorms. Climate is becoming more extreme. Last year, freak storm destroyed many open-field farms around me. My greenhouse was fine.

ALHAJI MUSA: But climate change is making farming harder overall. Droughts are longer, floods are worse, pests are increasing. Technology helps us cope, but the fundamental problem needs government action—reforestation, water conservation, climate policy.

FACILITATOR: What role should government play in supporting agricultural technology adoption?

[Animated discussion, multiple people speaking]

SAMUEL: Subsidize agricultural technology! Just like they subsidize fertilizer, they should subsidize irrigation equipment, processing machines, cold storage.

FATIMA: Tax exemptions for agricultural equipment imports. Currently, we pay duties and VAT. Remove those taxes!

MRS. CHIDINMA: Invest in rural infrastructure—electricity, internet, roads. These are foundations for technology.

BUNMI: Low-interest loans specifically for agric technology. Maybe 5% interest, 10-year repayment period. Current bank rates are too high.

CHIAMAKA: Government should buy from local producers! All these school feeding programs, military contracts, hospital supplies—mandate them to buy from Nigerian farmers and processors. That guarantees market.

YUSUF: Enforce agricultural policies. Ban imported rice, but then make sure smuggling is stopped. Otherwise, honest millers who invested in technology can't compete with smuggled cheap rice.

OKE: Create demonstration farms showing different technologies. Farmers are skeptical—"Does this really work?" Show them working examples, they'll adopt.

ALHAJI MUSA: Bring back functional extension services. Train extension officers in modern agricultural technology. Deploy them to rural areas to teach farmers.

FACILITATOR: Are any of you collaborating with each other or with tech companies?

MRS. CHIDINMA: Yes! We formed an agritech community—about 15 of us across Nigeria. We share knowledge, share suppliers, even share customers sometimes. If I can't fulfill order, I refer to another member.

BUNMI: I'm partnering with a logistics tech company. They provide cold trucks and optimization software. I focus on farming, they handle distribution. Partnership is working well.

SAMUEL: I work with a vet tech startup. They provide telemedicine for my chickens—I send photos or videos, they diagnose and advise. Saves me money on vet visits.

CHIAMAKA: We need more partnerships—farmers, tech companies, processors, distributors. Value chain integration is the future.

FACILITATOR: Looking ahead, what innovations do you anticipate or hope for?

MRS. CHIDINMA: AI for agriculture—predictive analytics for pest outbreak, yield forecasting, market price prediction. We're working on building such models.

SAMUEL: Drone technology for large farms—surveying, spraying, monitoring. Still too expensive now, but prices will come down.

BUNMI: Vertical farming—growing more food in less space, fully controlled environment. Perfect for urban areas.

FATIMA: Blockchain for agricultural supply chain. Imagine if every product can be traced from farm to table. That builds trust and eliminates fraud.

OKE: Genetic improvement combined with technology. Better fish breeds, better crop varieties, optimized through data.

YUSUF: Aggregation platforms connecting small farmers to big buyers. Technology can organize fragmented sector.

ALHAJI MUSA: [smiles] I hope for simpler technology. Things that old man like me can use easily. Not everything needs to be app and sensor. Sometimes simple, well-designed tool is best innovation.

[Others nod and laugh appreciatively]

FACILITATOR: Wonderful. Final thoughts—what gives you hope despite the challenges?

FATIMA: Young people are returning to agriculture! My two nephews, both university graduates, are farming now. They bring fresh ideas, technology savvy. Future is bright.

MRS. CHIDINMA: Nigeria needs to feed itself and can feed Africa. We have land, we have people, we have ingenuity. With right support, agricultural transformation is possible.

SAMUEL: I'm making profit. Despite all challenges, my business is growing. That's proof it works.

BUNMI: Seeing other farmers succeed inspires me. If they can do it, I can do it. We learn from each other.

OKE: Consumer awareness is growing. People want quality, traceable, safe food. They're willing to pay for it. That creates market for those of us using technology.

CHIAMAKA: Innovation is in Nigerian DNA. We've always found ways to overcome obstacles. Agricultural technology is just latest example.

YUSUF: Government is slowly paying attention. It's not enough yet, but there's movement. With continued advocacy, things will improve.

ALHAJI MUSA: At 56, I thought I was too old to change. But I've changed, and my farm is better for it. If old man can learn new tricks, anyone can. [others applaud]

FACILITATOR: Thank you all. Your experiences and insights are invaluable for understanding agricultural innovation in Nigeria.

---

[End of FGD]

KEY THEMES IDENTIFIED:
- Problem-driven adoption (specific crises/losses as catalysts)
- Capital access as primary barrier (high costs, unfavorable loan terms)
- Lack of collateral (especially for Northern farmers with communal land)
- Power costs undermining technology benefits
- Technical knowledge gaps and learning through informal channels (YouTube)
- Maintenance and spare parts challenges (imported vs local equipment trade-offs)
- Customs and importation difficulties
- Collapsed agricultural extension services
- Market access improvements through quality and consistency
- Post-harvest losses and infrastructure gaps (roads, cold chain)
- Climate change adaptation through technology
- Government policy wishlist (subsidies, infrastructure, enforcement, procurement)
- Emerging collaborations and partnerships
- Generational shifts (young people bringing tech to farming)
- Optimism despite challenges
- Diversity of innovations (from simple mechanization to AI and blockchain)
- Gender representation and unique challenges
