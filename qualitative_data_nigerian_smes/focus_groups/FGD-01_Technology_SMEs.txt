FOCUS GROUP DISCUSSION TRANSCRIPT
===================================

FGD ID: FGD-01
Sector: Technology SMEs
Location: CcHub, Yaba, Lagos
Date: September 12, 2024
Duration: 135 minutes
Facilitator: Dr. Amaka Eze
Number of Participants: 7

PARTICIPANTS:
1. Chidi (M, 34) - Software Development, Lagos - Cloud & AI
2. Kemi (F, 29) - Fintech, Lagos - Mobile Payments
3. Bola (M, 38) - EdTech, Ibadan - E-learning Platform
4. Hauwa (F, 31) - Healthtech, Abuja - Telemedicine
5. Segun (M, 42) - Cybersecurity, Lagos - Security Solutions
6. Zainab (F, 27) - E-commerce Tech, Kano - Online Marketplace
7. Tolu (M, 36) - Data Analytics, Port Harcourt - Business Intelligence

---

[Introduction, consent, ground rules - 15 minutes]

FACILITATOR: Thank you all for joining us today. We're here to discuss your experiences with innovation adoption in your tech businesses, the constraints you face, and strategies for success. Let's start with a broad question: What are the biggest challenges you face in adopting and implementing new technologies in Nigeria?

CHIDI: [jumps in immediately] Infrastructure! Everything starts and ends with infrastructure. You cannot do cloud computing when internet is unreliable. You cannot run AI models when power keeps failing. It's like—

SEGUN: [interrupting] No, no, I disagree. Infrastructure is a problem, yes, but the bigger issue is talent. Nigeria has many IT graduates, but they don't have practical skills. I spend 6 months training someone, then Google or Facebook hires them.

KEMI: Both are right, but you're missing the elephant in the room—regulation! [others nod] CBN, SEC, NITDA—everyone wants to regulate fintech, but nobody understands what we're doing. I've spent over a year just getting licenses.

BOLA: Can I add something? For me, the biggest challenge is market education. I'm building e-learning platforms, but Nigerian parents don't trust online education. They want physical teachers, physical classrooms. Changing mindsets is harder than building technology.

FACILITATOR: So we have infrastructure, talent, regulation, market education. Are these interconnected?

HAUWA: Very interconnected. Look at my telemedicine platform. Infrastructure—many areas have poor internet, so video consultations fail. Regulation—Medical Council is suspicious of online doctors. Market—patients don't trust seeing doctors on phone. Talent—I can't find developers who understand healthcare domain. Everything is linked.

ZAINAB: And add cost to that. I'm in Kano, developing an online marketplace for Northern traders. Everything is more expensive because we're not in Lagos. To get good developers, I have to pay Lagos salaries. Internet costs more. Even training programs are all in Lagos or Abuja.

TOLU: [laughs] But at least you don't have security issues like us in Port Harcourt! Kidnapping, militancy—it's hard to attract talent or investors when they're afraid to visit.

FACILITATOR: Let's explore each of these in depth. Starting with infrastructure—how specifically does it constrain your innovation?

CHIDI: Let me give concrete example. My company does cloud solutions. We host applications on AWS. Our clients are in Lagos, Abuja, Port Harcourt, everywhere. When we need to deploy updates or debug issues, we need fast internet. In Lagos, I have maybe 70% reliable internet if I have two providers. But when I visit clients in other states, sometimes I'm working from hotel with 2G speed. I can't download necessary files, can't push updates. Projects that should take 2 hours take 2 days.

Power is worse. AWS costs us dollars—expensive but reliable. But our office, our developers' homes—no power. Everyone working on generators or UPS. Productivity drops. One of my developers, his generator failed, he lost a whole day's work because his laptop battery died mid-coding.

SEGUN: Exactly! And for cybersecurity, I need to monitor systems 24/7. How can I do that when power is failing? I have to over-invest in backup systems—generators, UPS, batteries. My infrastructure cost is almost 40% of my budget. In a normal country, maybe it would be 10%.

KEMI: For fintech, infrastructure means we can't reach rural areas. Our mobile payment platform needs internet. Village woman with smartphone but no internet can't use our service. That's millions of potential users locked out. Meanwhile, M-Pesa in Kenya works even on basic phones with USSD. We're constrained by Nigerian telecoms infrastructure.

FACILITATOR: So infrastructure limits your market reach, increases costs, reduces productivity. How do you cope?

TOLU: We cope by accepting lower profit margins. I do data analytics for oil and gas companies. They pay well, but my costs are high. In other countries, similar companies might have 40-50% profit margins. We're lucky to get 20% because of infrastructure costs.

HAUWA: We also limit our ambition. My original vision was to connect every Nigerian to healthcare via telemedicine. Reality? We focus on urban middle class with smartphones and internet. We've abandoned rural areas for now. It's not by choice; it's necessity.

BOLA: [nodding vigorously] Same! EdTech should be democratizing education—reaching the poorest students. But they don't have devices, don't have internet. So we target private schools and middle-class families. We're innovating, yes, but not for those who need it most.

ZAINAB: This is painful truth. Technology is supposed to be equalizer, but in Nigeria, it's becoming divider. Those with access leap ahead, those without are left behind.

FACILITATOR: Powerful observation. Let's shift to talent and skills. Segun mentioned losing trained staff to big companies. Is this a shared experience?

SEGUN: [sighs] It's the story of Nigerian tech. We're basically training institutes for Google, Microsoft, Meta. They come with their recruiters, offering three times our salary, visa to relocate abroad. How do we compete?

CHIDI: [defensive] But can we blame the young people? If you're 26, someone offers you $120,000 and life in California versus ₦5 million in Lagos with no power, which would you choose?

KEMI: [firmly] We can't blame them, but we can change our approach. I've stopped trying to compete on salary. Instead, I offer equity. I tell my developers: "You can be employee at Google, or you can be co-owner here. When this company grows, you become millionaire." Three of my early developers now own 15% of the company. They're committed because it's theirs.

TOLU: That's smart, but it only works if the company actually grows. Many startups fail. Your equity is worthless.

KEMI: True, but it's better than losing everyone to foreign companies. Also, I focus on culture. We have flexible work, learning budget, latest equipment. Google has bigger salary, but we have family atmosphere, sense of mission. Some people value that.

HAUWA: I think the talent issue is also about education system. Universities teach outdated curricula. Students graduate knowing Java from 15 years ago but not modern frameworks. We have to re-train them completely. Why can't universities partner with tech companies to teach relevant skills?

BOLA: [laughs bitterly] Because our education system is stuck in the 20th century! Lecturers who've never built a real software are teaching software engineering. It's theoretical, not practical.

ZAINAB: In Kano, it's even worse. We barely have CS graduates. Most tech-interested young people go to Lagos. We're trying to build ecosystem in Northern Nigeria, but brain drain is killing us.

FACILITATOR: So talent challenges include: poaching by foreign companies, poor university curricula, geographic concentration in Lagos. What solutions are emerging?

CHIDI: There's a growing movement of tech training bootcamps—Decagon, AltSchool, others. They train people intensively for 6 months in practical skills. It's helping, but they're expensive for many young people.

SEGUN: Remote work is also helping. After COVID, we realized we can hire talent from anywhere. I have developers in Calabar, Kaduna, Jos—paying them less than Lagos rates, but more than they'd get locally. They're happy, I'm happy.

KEMI: But remote work has challenges. How do you build company culture? How do you ensure productivity? We tried full remote and had issues with accountability.

HAUWA: Hybrid model works for us. Team comes to office 2-3 days per week for collaboration, works remotely other days. Best of both worlds.

FACILITATOR: Let's talk about regulation. Kemi, you mentioned spending a year on licensing. Can others relate?

KEMI: [passionate] It's a nightmare! For fintech, you need CBN approval, NITDA approval, partnership with licensed bank, data protection registration. Each agency has different requirements, different timelines. Nobody talks to each other. You submit same documents to five different places.

BOLA: And they don't understand technology! I'm building e-learning platform. Is that education? Is that technology? Is that media? Which ministry regulates me? Nobody knows! So multiple agencies claim jurisdiction.

CHIDI: [animated] I had a regulator ask me: "Where is your data center?" I said, "We use AWS cloud." He said, "But where is the physical server in Nigeria?" I explained cloud computing for 30 minutes. He still didn't understand. He wanted me to show him a physical server room in Nigeria!

[Group laughter]

ZAINAB: It's because regulators are from different generation. They think technology means desktop computers and CD-ROMs. They don't understand cloud, AI, blockchain, APIs—these concepts are foreign to them.

TOLU: But to be fair, some regulation is necessary. We've had scams—MMM, get-rich-quick apps. People lost billions. Regulators are trying to protect citizens. The problem is they're using 1990s tools to regulate 2024 technology.

HAUWA: Exactly! Health regulation is important—we can't have quacks pretending to be doctors online. But the regulatory process should be modern. Everything is paper-based, physical submissions, waiting months. Why not online portal, automated approvals for low-risk categories?

KEMI: We need a sandbox approach like UK, Singapore. Let startups test innovations in controlled environment. If it works and is safe, then full approval. Don't strangle innovation with bureaucracy.

FACILITATOR: Has anyone successfully navigated the regulatory landscape? Any strategies?

SEGUN: Hire a regulatory consultant. Seriously. I spent 8 months failing to get approvals on my own. Hired a consultant who knows people in NITDA and other agencies. Got approval in 6 weeks. Cost me ₦2 million, but worth it.

KEMI: That's part of the problem, though. You need to "know people" or pay consultants. It's not transparent. Small startups without money or connections can't compete.

BOLA: We formed an industry association—EdTech Nigeria. We engage regulators collectively, educate them, advocate for better policies. There's strength in numbers. Regulators listen more to an association than to one small startup.

FACILITATOR: Good strategies. Let's discuss funding. How do you finance your innovation?

HAUWA: [laughs] Personal savings and prayers! Honestly, funding is brutal. Banks don't understand tech businesses. "Where is your collateral? What assets do we seize if you fail?" They don't understand that our assets are software, data, algorithms.

CHIDI: I've pitched to over 50 investors—angels, VCs, everyone. Maybe 80% rejection rate. Comments like "Too early stage," "Not enough traction," "Nigerian market too risky." It's discouraging.

ZAINAB: And being in Kano, investors don't even visit. Lagos-based VCs don't look beyond Lagos and maybe Abuja. Northern Nigeria doesn't exist to them. We have to be 10 times better to get noticed.

TOLU: Gender bias too. I've seen it. My co-founder is female—brilliant engineer. When we pitch together, investors direct technical questions to me, even though she's the technical lead. They assume woman can't be technical.

KEMI: [visibly frustrated] Oh, don't get me started! I've had investors ask me: "As a woman, how will you manage stress?" "Do you plan to have children soon?" Would they ask male founders this? I've learned to address it head-on now. "Yes, I'm a woman. Yes, I might have children. That doesn't affect my ability to build a billion-naira company."

[Others applaud]

FACILITATOR: So funding challenges include: bank unwillingness, investor risk aversion, geographic bias, gender bias. Where do you actually get funding?

CHIDI: Friends and family for early stage. I raised ₦15 million from relatives and former colleagues. Then we got into an accelerator—CcHub—they gave us ₦5 million grant plus mentorship.

BOLA: Bootstrapping. We're profitable from year one—rare in tech, I know. We charged customers from day one, reinvested profits. Slow growth, but sustainable. No investors telling us what to do.

SEGUN: International investors are more willing than local ones. I got funding from a European impact investor focused on African tech. They understand the market better than Nigerian banks, ironically.

HAUWA: Government grants exist but very competitive. I've applied to five grant programs, got one—₦3 million from Lagos State. It helped, but not sufficient. We need millions more to scale.

KEMI: Diaspora funding is underutilized. Nigerians abroad want to invest in Nigeria but don't know how. We need better platforms connecting diaspora money with local startups.

FACILITATOR: What about government support beyond grants? Are there policies or programs helping you?

[Long pause]

TOLU: [finally] The silence speaks volumes. [laughter]

CHIDI: Honestly, government is neutral at best, obstructive at worst. They talk about digital economy, fourth industrial revolution—nice speeches. Actual support? Minimal.

ZAINAB: Northern states are trying. Kaduna had a startup fund, Kano has innovation hubs. But budgets are tiny compared to need. And programs change when government changes.

BOLA: Federal government's tax policies kill us. We're paying same taxes as oil companies, but we're not profitable yet! We need tax holidays for first 5 years, or tax credits for R&D investment.

HAUWA: NITDA has some good programs—capacity building, grants. But it's drop in the ocean. What we need is infrastructure—fix power, fix internet, fix roads. That's the biggest support government can give.

KEMI: And stable policy! Exchange rate volatility is killing us. Our costs are dollar-denominated—AWS, software licenses, even some salaries. When naira crashes, our costs double. How do we plan business in that environment?

SEGUN: We need government to be early adopter of our technologies. Government contracts could sustain many startups. But procurement is corrupt, bureaucratic. Small tech companies can't compete with big foreign contractors.

FACILITATOR: Let's talk about successes. Despite all these challenges, you're all still in business, growing. What's working?

CHIDI: [smiles] Nigerian resilience! We're survivors. We find ways. Generator fails? We work from car using hotspot. Internet fails? We work at night when it's better. We adapt constantly.

KEMI: Community is our strength. Tech ecosystem in Lagos is collaborative. We share knowledge, share contacts, help each other. Competition exists, but collaboration is stronger.

BOLA: Customer need is real. Education problems in Nigeria are massive. Businesses need solutions. Market is huge. That keeps us going even when times are hard.

HAUWA: Impact motivates us. When a mother in Maiduguri tells me her child got quality healthcare through our platform—something impossible before—that's worth more than profit. We're solving real problems.

ZAINAB: Young people are embracing technology. Gen Z, millennials—they want digital solutions. They trust apps more than traditional systems. This demographic shift favors us.

TOLU: Data is the new oil, as they say. Nigerian businesses are realizing data-driven decisions are better than gut feeling. Demand for our analytics services is growing 50% yearly.

SEGUN: Cybersecurity awareness is rising. After some high-profile hacks, companies now budget for security. Five years ago, nobody wanted to pay for cybersecurity. Now it's essential.

FACILITATOR: What innovations do you see on the horizon? What's next?

CHIDI: AI is the future. We're integrating AI into everything—customer service bots, predictive analytics, process automation. Nigeria will be AI-powered in 10 years.

KEMI: Embedded finance. Every business will have financial services built in—e-commerce with instant credit, logistics with insurance, healthcare with payment plans. We're building those integrations.

BOLA: Personalized learning using AI. Every student gets customized curriculum based on their pace, learning style, gaps. This is possible with technology.

HAUWA: Virtual reality for training doctors and nurses. VR surgical simulations, emergency response training. We're exploring partnerships with medical schools.

ZAINAB: Voice and low-bandwidth solutions for Northern Nigeria. Not everyone types English well, not everyone has 4G. Voice-activated, low-data apps will unlock millions of users.

TOLU: Blockchain for transparency. Supply chain tracking, contract management, identity verification—blockchain can solve Nigeria's trust deficit.

SEGUN: Quantum computing—very far future, but coming. We need to prepare for post-quantum cryptography.

FACILITATOR: If you could change one thing to accelerate technology innovation in Nigeria, what would it be?

CHIDI: Fix power! Stable 24-hour electricity would transform everything.

KEMI: Regulatory clarity and speed. Modern, tech-savvy regulators.

BOLA: Education reform. Teach coding in primary school, modernize university curricula.

HAUWA: Infrastructure—power, internet, roads. Basics first.

ZAINAB: Geographic equity. Invest in tech ecosystems outside Lagos.

TOLU: Access to capital. More VCs, lower interest rates, longer-term patient capital.

SEGUN: Government as customer. Procurement reform to favor local tech companies.

FACILITATOR: Final question: If you were advising a new tech entrepreneur, what would you say?

CHIDI: Start. Don't wait for perfect conditions. They'll never come in Nigeria. Start with what you have.

KEMI: Build for Nigeria, but think global. Our solutions can work across Africa and beyond.

BOLA: Cash flow is king. Profitability matters more than vanity metrics.

HAUWA: Solve real problems. Don't copy Silicon Valley. Build for Nigerian realities.

ZAINAB: Be resilient. You'll face 100 obstacles. Overcome them one by one.

TOLU: Build a strong team. Co-founders, early employees—choose wisely. People matter more than ideas.

SEGUN: [laughs] Get a good therapist! Entrepreneurship is stressful. Mental health matters.

[Group laughter and agreement]

FACILITATOR: Thank you all. This has been incredibly rich. Your insights will be valuable for understanding tech innovation in Nigeria.

---

[End of FGD]

KEY THEMES IDENTIFIED:
- Infrastructure (power, internet) as fundamental constraint
- Talent challenges: poaching, education gaps, geographic concentration
- Regulatory complexity and knowledge gap
- Funding challenges with multiple dimensions of bias
- Geographic inequality (Lagos-centrism)
- Gender bias in investment and operations
- Community and collaboration as coping mechanism
- Resilience and adaptation as core capability
- Market demand as motivator despite challenges
- Optimism about future despite current constraints
- Diversity of next-generation innovations (AI, blockchain, VR)
- Consensus on infrastructure as top priority for policy
